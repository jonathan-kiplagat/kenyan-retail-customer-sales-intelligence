# Defence Preparation: Likely Questions and Answers

Short, evidence-based answers to the questions an examiner is most likely to ask. The numbers can be checked in the
linked notebooks and reports.

---

## Data

**1. Why did you use synthetic data? Isn't that less valuable than real data?**
Real customer-level retail data cannot be published in a public repository without privacy risk. Synthetic data let
me share the whole pipeline. It also makes the analysis checkable: the generating rules are documented, so I can
confirm that RFM really recovered the hidden loyal segment, and that cleaning found every injected error. The
limitation is that the figures are not market evidence, and I state that on every report.

**2. How do you know the data is realistic?**
Prices and costs follow Kenyan shelf prices. Demand follows real retail patterns: a December peak, Saturday and
payday effects, school-term clothing demand, and the **real gazetted public holidays**. The headline shapes match
what retail analysts expect: right-skewed baskets (mean 822 vs median 450), 77% one-off customers, and the top 20% of
customers producing about 60% of revenue. Notebook 02 validates all of this.

**3. Why exactly 3,000 rows when the clean data has 2,988?**
The assignment fixes the raw file at 3,000 rows. Twelve of them are injected duplicates. They are part of the raw
reality that cleaning must remove, so 2,988 is the correct clean count.

**4. You removed the KNBS data. Doesn't that weaken the project?**
It was removed at the project owner's request, to keep the scope on internal sales data. Public holidays remain as
real external data and are used in the EDA and the forecast features. I list macroeconomic drivers as a limitation.

## Cleaning

**5. How do you know your cleaning was correct?**
Every injected issue was logged at generation time. After cleaning, I compared the output with that log and with
the true data, cell by cell: **all 126 issues were detected**. The only differences left are 21 customer IDs and 7
payment methods with no evidence, which I flagged rather than guessed.

**6. Why not fill missing payment methods with the most common value (M-Pesa)?**
That would inflate M-Pesa's share and invent facts. Payment is captured once per receipt, so I copied it from another
line of the same receipt where one existed (3 cases). The other 7 are labelled "Unknown".

**7. Why keep outliers?**
An outlier is only removed if it is an error. Quantity 999 contradicted the receipt total, so it was corrected; left
in, it would have added KES 1.44M of phantom revenue. The 60-bottle water purchase and the electronics lines
reconcile with their totals, so they are real sales. The skew is handled with medians and non-parametric tests.

## Analysis and SQL

**8. Why is average order value calculated per order and not per line?**
A receipt with three products is one customer visit. Calculating per line would split it into three small "orders"
and understate basket size.

**9. Revenue grew 8.8%. Isn't that good news?**
Partly. Units fell 4.7% and orders grew only 3.8%. Most of the growth came from the 4% price review and a richer
basket mix. If prices stop rising, revenue will stall, so I recommend tracking units alongside revenue.

**10. Why is your database normalized, with a separate `orders` table?**
Date, time, store, customer and payment method belong to the receipt, not to each line. Storing them once in
`orders` (2,650 rows) prevents contradictions, such as two lines of the same receipt with different payment methods.
Money columns are generated by the database, so they cannot disagree with quantity and price.

**11. How did you verify that SQL and Python agree?**
Query Q3.6 compares the database's generated revenue, discount and profit with the Python-cleaned values on all
2,988 lines: zero mismatches, KES 2,179,214.72 in both. The at-risk count (249) and the RFM counts (68 Champions)
also match across SQL, Python and Power BI.

**12. Why fixed bands for RFM recency and frequency instead of quintiles?**
77% of customers have one order, so frequency quintiles would give identical customers different scores at random.
For recency, quintiles produced a band of 255–406 days for score 3, which labelled a customer silent for ten months
as "Loyal". Fixed bands (last quarter, half-year, year) are both fairer and easier for managers to read.

## Statistics

**13. Why non-parametric tests?**
Units per line and order values are discrete and heavily skewed, and Shapiro-Wilk rejected normality in every group.
Mann-Whitney and Kruskal-Wallis compare ranks, so a few large electronics baskets cannot dominate the result. Welch
tests on log values are reported as support.

**14. The store difference is significant. Should each store have its own strategy?**
No. The p-value (0.017) says the difference is probably real, but the effect size (ε² = 0.003) says the store
explains 0.3% of the variation in order value. Only Mombasa vs Westlands differ. Statistical significance is not
business significance.

**15. Do promotions cause higher sales?**
I can't claim that. Promotion lines sell about half a unit more per line (95% CI 0.21–0.93) in every category. But
campaigns were not randomly assigned, and they coincide with seasonal peaks. I recommend a controlled store-level
trial to measure the causal effect.

**16. Why use Holm correction?**
Running 15 pairwise store tests at α = 0.05 gives about a 54% chance of at least one false positive. Holm controls
the family-wise error rate, and it is more powerful than Bonferroni.

## Models

**17. Your forecast beats naive by only 5%. Is it useful?**
I report it honestly as not conclusive on six test months. Its value is that the error is known (MAE KES 17,500,
16.5%), so the forecast comes with a realistic range. I also give a planning figure that corrects for January's
known weakness. With more history, a seasonal model should beat both.

**18. Why did you choose Holt over the Random Forest?**
Holt had the lower validation MAE, and the choice was frozen before the test period. On test it was again slightly
better on MAE (17,500 vs 17,678), and it is much simpler to explain and maintain. The Random Forest had a lower RMSE,
so it makes fewer huge misses. With more data it is a reasonable challenger.

**19. How did you prevent data leakage?**
- **Chronological splits only.**
- **Past-only features:** lags are shifted, and the store level is recomputed at each forecast origin.
- **A unit test proves it:** multiplying all future revenue by 10 leaves the forecast unchanged.
- **Churn features stop at the snapshot date,** and labels use only the next 6 months.
- **Model choices were frozen** in `model_selection.json` before the test evaluation.

**20. Why did a simple rule win the churn model selection?**
In cross-validation the three approaches were statistically tied: only 33 training customers returned, so the
estimates are noisy. The one-standard-error rule picks the simplest model in that case. On the later test period,
logistic regression ranked customers slightly better (AUC +0.037, CI just above zero), so I use it as a priority
score and recommend promoting it if the advantage holds.

**21. Why not use accuracy for churn?**
About 80% of established customers did not return. A model that flags everyone would be 80% accurate and useless. I
use AUC for ranking, and precision for campaign cost: 93% of the customers the rule flags really did not return.

**22. Why 180 days?**
It comes from the data. Of 841 observed repeat-purchase gaps, 90% were 173 days or shorter, so silence beyond 180
days is unlike nine in ten genuine returns. The caveat is that longer gaps cannot be observed in a two-year window.

## Dashboard and business

**23. Why does the date slicer not change the customer page?**
RFM segment and at-risk status are measured at one point in time (31 Dec 2025) over each customer's whole history.
Filtering them by date would change their meaning. That page uses a preferred-store slicer instead.

**24. If management could act on only one recommendation, which should it be?**
The win-back campaign. It targets KES 542K of past spend, uses a list that proved 93% precise on later data, and
includes a control group, so its value can be measured. Protecting clothing margins comes a close second, because
clothing earns 46% of profit.

**25. What would you do with more time?**
- Run a basket analysis to design the Mombasa bundles.
- Fit a seasonal forecast once there are three years of data.
- Estimate customer lifetime value.
- Run randomized promotion trials.
- Automate the pipeline from the database to Power BI.
